Dental emergency after-hours FAQs

What counts as a dental emergency after hours?

True after-hours emergencies: severe or worsening pain, facial swelling, trauma or knocked-out tooth, uncontrolled bleeding, signs of infection (fever with tooth pain). Lost fillings, dull aches, and cosmetic issues can almost always wait until morning. Your practice defines the exact list — Sunny follows it.

Who should answer dental emergency calls at night?

Every call should be answered — the question is by whom. Options: your cell (exhausting), a traditional answering service (message-taking with callback delay), or an AI receptionist like Sunny that triages immediately and pages your on-call dentist only for true emergencies.

How does Sunny triage a dental emergency?

She asks targeted questions based on your protocol — pain level, swelling, bleeding, trauma — then follows your rules: page the on-call number immediately for true emergencies, or book the next available slot for urgent-but-stable cases. Non-urgent callers get booked without waking anyone.

Will Sunny wake me up for non-emergencies?

No — that's the point of the protocol. You define what pages you and what doesn't. A lost crown at midnight gets a morning appointment; a swollen face with fever gets you on the phone in seconds.

Can patients reach the on-call dentist directly?

Only through your escalation rules. Sunny never hands out the on-call number — she bridges the urgent call to it herself, so your personal number stays private and only true emergencies come through.

What happens with emergency calls on weekends and holidays?

Same as weeknights: Sunny answers 24/7/365, triages against your protocol, and escalates true emergencies to whoever is on call that day. Your holiday coverage is configured per-date in onboarding.

Does Sunny give medical advice?

No. Sunny never diagnoses or prescribes — she gathers information, applies your triage rules, and routes. Anything clinical goes straight to your on-call dentist.

What should a dental office's after-hours emergency protocol include?

Four things: which symptoms count as urgent, what the caller is told first (life-threatening symptoms go to 911 or urgent care), how the on-call dentist is reached for true emergencies, and a written record of every call for the morning. Sunny follows the protocol you approve — she doesn't improvise it.

How are emergency calls routed to the on-call dentist?

By your rules. If a caller's situation matches your urgent criteria, Sunny flags it for the on-call route you've set and makes sure the caller knows the next step. Non-urgent callers are captured for a morning callback instead of waking anyone at 2 AM.

What's the difference between an urgent and a non-urgent after-hours call?

Urgent means uncontrolled bleeding, swelling that affects breathing or vision, trauma to the face or jaw, or pain that can't be managed — those need care now. A chipped tooth with no pain or a lost filling is usually a first-appointment-in-the-morning call. Your protocol draws the line; Sunny applies it consistently.

Should emergency callers ever be told to call 911?

Yes — for anything life-threatening, like swelling that is closing an airway or heavy bleeding that won't stop, the right instruction is 911 or urgent care immediately, not a callback queue. Sunny says exactly that, and still flags the call for your office.
