The math, step by step

Most calculators hide their workings and print the scariest number possible. Here's every step of ours, live with your numbers:

  1. Calls per month: 100 calls/week × 4.33 weeks (52 ÷ 12) = 433.33 calls a month.
  2. Missed per month: 130 of those calls go unanswered (30%).
  3. Missed calls from EXISTING patients: 97.5 — counted as $0 lost. An Open Dental study of 8,887 real calls found 97% of existing patients who hit a busy phone call back within the hour. A few will slip away in real life, so counting them all as safe makes this estimate run conservative on purpose.
  4. Missed calls from NEW patients: 32.5. New patients have no loyalty to your office yet — you're one of several tabs open in their browser.
  5. New patients who never call back: 16.25 of them (your 50% assumption from the slider above).
  6. Estimated loss: 16.25 patients × $1,000 first-year value = $16,250/month, or $195,000/year.

Think step 5 is too harsh? It might be — that's why it's a slider and not a footnote. Even at 25%, most offices find the number is still the most expensive line they never see.

Where these numbers come from

  • 30–38% of in-hours calls missed — benchmark from Dental Economics, widely cited in dental practice-management reporting.

  • $850–$1,500 first-year value of a new patient — range across published dental industry estimates.
  • The never-call-back share for new patients — no published study we're willing to stand behind, so it's labeled an assumption above and left in your hands.

Honest FAQ

Where do these numbers come from?

Three published sources: the Dental Economics benchmark (practices miss 30–38% of in-hours calls), the Open Dental study of 8,887 calls (97% of existing patients call back within the hour), and industry estimates of new-patient value ($850–$1,500 in year one). The one figure with no study behind it — how many missed new patients never retry — is labeled an assumption on the calculator itself, and you set it.

Why don't you count missed calls from existing patients?

Because the evidence says they call back — 97% of them, within the hour. Counting them as lost would roughly triple the headline number, and it would be wrong. The real, quiet loss is new patients: no relationship, no loyalty, and a list of other offices one tap away.
